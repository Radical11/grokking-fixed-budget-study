## 2. The problem: delayed learning on new questions

Target: 60 seconds; start at 0:20.

A neural network is a learning program that adjusts numbers inside it. Think of practising maths questions and then trying questions with different numbers. Memorization means doing well on the practice examples. Generalization means doing well on questions excluded from practice. Grokking is the delayed improvement on new questions after the practice answers are already good. The student example is an analogy for the measured pattern. Our objectives are to measure how much that delay varies, compare training settings and ask whether a limited budget is better spent on one attempt or several fresh starts. A budget here is a number of learning steps. Existing research established grokking and restart theory; our contribution is this controlled experimental comparison.

## 3. A controlled experiment with 120 main attempts

Target: 65 seconds; start at 1:20.

The task is modular addition: add two numbers and keep the remainder after division by 31. For example, 30 plus 4 leaves remainder 3. All 961 number pairs are generated by Python. We compare practice sets containing 40 percent or 60 percent of the pairs, and three weight-decay settings. Weight decay gently shrinks the model's adjustable numbers. The remaining pairs are reserved for checking new-question accuracy. The split seed fixes the way pairs are divided; an initialization seed chooses repeatable starting weights. Twenty starting seeds in each of six settings give 120 main attempts. The same small transformer, a type of neural network, is used throughout. Python and PyTorch train it; R calculates the statistics. Nine setup runs select the reference setting and are excluded from main-study inference.

## 4. One clear rule for success

Target: 65 seconds; start at 2:25.

An update is one learning step using the full practice set. Every 25 updates we check the percentage of correct practice and new answers. Success requires at least 99 percent practice accuracy and 95 percent new-question accuracy at three checks in a row. T is the update at the third check. It varies between starting seeds, so it is a random variable. Every main attempt continues to the cap C of 30000. If success has not happened, its eventual T remains unknown. For average comparisons we define U as the smaller of T and C. That is a capped outcome, not a claim that an unfinished attempt learned at the cap. A completed training run and an observed generalization event are different things. Three checks measure first confirmation; later performance may fall.

## 6. Full results across the six settings

Target: 65 seconds; start at 4:25.

Each setting contains twenty independent starts. The bars show average capped time U, which retains unfinished attempts at the limit. The table also shows the number of observed generalization events. At 40 percent practice data and decay 0.3, none of twenty attempts succeeds within the cap. Its mean U is therefore 30000, but the true eventual learning times are unknown. At 60 percent data and decay 3, all twenty succeed and the mean capped time is 1430 updates. These results show that both the amount of practice data and the training control matter in this fixed task. They do not prove a universally best setting for every network. The reference remains 60 percent data and decay 1, chosen through separate pilot runs.

## 7. Waiting time varies even at the reference setting

Target: 55 seconds; start at 5:30.

The reference, or baseline, setting has twenty observed events. The mean waiting time is 5481.25 updates, the median is 4025 and the sample standard deviation is about 5548. The median is the middle of the sorted values; standard deviation measures spread around the average. The histogram shows a long right tail, so an average alone hides some of the variability. All twenty succeed by the cap, but that is a sample result. The exact 95 percent interval for the population success probability is 83.2 to 100 percent. A bootstrap, which repeatedly resamples these twenty observations, gives an approximate 95 percent interval of 3596 to 8224 for mean U. Neither interval makes a guarantee about the next attempt.

## 8. Which distribution describes the waiting times?

Target: 50 seconds; start at 6:25.

A probability distribution is a mathematical description of how waiting times are spread. We compare three candidate families: Exponential, Gamma and Weibull. They are fitted to the selected reference setting, which has twenty observed events and zero unfinished runs. The method can include an unfinished run through its probability of exceeding the cap, but this particular baseline fit has no such observations. Evaluations happen every 25 updates and three-check confirmation adds a 50-update lag, so the likelihood accounts for that recording grid. AIC compares fit while accounting for the number of model parameters. Gamma has the smallest AIC, about 253.96, among these three candidates. Twenty observations and a small AIC difference do not establish a true universal timing law.

## 9. Statistical comparisons and their limits

Target: 55 seconds; start at 7:15.

The syllabus methods answer different questions. One-way ANOVA compares the three decay groups at 60 percent data. Two-factor ANOVA compares data fraction, decay and their interaction across all six settings. Interaction asks whether the effect of one control changes with the other. The fraction and decay comparisons have small p-values; the interaction is not clearly supported in this dataset. Multiple regression describes capped time using both fraction and log decay. Its R-squared is 0.630, describing the proportion of sample variation explained by that fitted model, not prediction accuracy on new experiments. Residuals, the observed times minus model predictions, fail a bell-shape diagnostic. Permutation checks shuffle suitable data under the null assumption, but they also require exchangeability. These are exploratory comparisons with assumptions and uncertainty.

## 10. Predictions for one attempt versus restarts

Target: 65 seconds; start at 8:10.

A restart begins training again with fresh starting weights. Let B be the total update budget and k the number of attempts. Each receives B divided by k updates. F of a time limit is the chance that one attempt succeeds by that limit. The chance that all independent attempts fail is the product of their individual failure chances. Subtract from one to get the chance that at least one succeeds. These predictions use the twenty main baseline runs, not the fresh validation runs. At B equal to 30000, predicted success is 100 percent, 99.8 percent and 99.9 percent for one, two and four attempts. The curves also show that behaviour depends on the total budget. Predictions at smaller budgets have not received fresh-seed validation.

## 11. Fresh starting seeds test the predictions

Target: 65 seconds; start at 9:15.

We then use new seeds that are separate from both pilot and main-study seeds. Ten independent budget attempts are performed for each policy. One, two and four training runs per attempt produce seventy constituent runs across thirty budget attempts. Every attempt receives exactly 30000 logical updates in total. All three policies succeed in all ten attempts. Each exact 95 percent interval is 69.2 to 100 percent, reflecting the small validation sample. This generous budget does not identify a clear winner and does not establish equivalence. We do not force a policy comparison test onto a table where every outcome is identical. Runtime is descriptive: concurrent CPU work and checkpoint recovery prevent a controlled wall-clock speed comparison. Equal logical update budgets do not imply equal total physical recovery cost.

## 12. What the completed study establishes

Target: 55 seconds; start at 10:20.

The study demonstrates measurable variation in learning time between random starts and substantial differences across the measured settings. It also gives a transparent budget comparison with a valid inconclusive policy result at the chosen cap. All 199 experimental training runs are retained: nine pilot, 120 main and seventy validation constituents. Among the 83 generalized main runs, 62 later drop below 95 percent new-question accuracy, so first confirmation is not permanent success. The evidence is conditional on one fixed split, a small architecture and a CPU environment. Future research can test smaller budgets, repeat different practice/test splits and measure success that stays high. Those extensions are separate from completing the current protocol. The reports, raw traces, analysis code and reproduction commands are available in the repository.

## 14. Appendix A: terms and a worked probability

Optional question slide.

Seed means a repeatable random starting point. Update means one adjustment of the model's weights. Generalization means good performance on questions excluded from practice. Censoring means that the time lies beyond the observation limit; it is not a failed software run. In a hypothetical example, two independent attempts each have a 60 percent success chance, so both fail with chance 0.4 times 0.4, or 0.16. At least one succeeds with chance 0.84. To choose between that policy and one longer run, we also need the longer run's probability. This example is for explanation and is not a measured result.
